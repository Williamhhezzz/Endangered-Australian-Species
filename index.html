Twenty Years of Wild Decline

Setting year 2000 wildlife populations at 100% (index 1.0), monitored wildlife fell by 62.6% over two decades (to 37.4% of baseline, or 0.374, by 2020). Two-thirds of this drop occurred in the first decade during the Millennium Drought (2001–2009), and populations never recovered after rains returned.

Legal Listing Lags Reality

Putting more species onto the national protection list (adding 94 new species after 2000 to reach 429 protected animals overall) has not stopped populations from falling in the wild. Nearly 80% of new listings were birds and mammals, whereas fast-vanishing frogs received almost no new listings until 2019.

The Collapse of Frogs & Reptiles

Comparing all four groups since 1985 reveals a stark divide. While monitored mammals (62% left) and birds (44% left) retained roughly half their baseline levels by 2022, cold-blooded reptiles (6%) and amphibians (3%) suffered near-total collapse due to drying wetlands, habitat loss, and an infectious amphibian skin fungus.

Why Some Sub-Groups Recover While Others Collapse

Trends vary sharply within each group since 2000. Marine mammals (+42.9%) and large mammals (+25.7%) grew under whaling bans and reserves. Conversely, land-breeding frogs (-95.2%) and freshwater reptiles (-76.5%) collapsed from drying habitats, shorebirds (-56.4%) lost Asian coastal stopovers, and mid-sized mammals (-47.9%) fell prey to cats and foxes.

Tasmania's Ocean Shield

Plotting percentage change since 2000 compares compact and large states fairly. Victoria (-62.3%) fell steepest from historic farmland clearing, while Northern Territory wildlife (-61.8%) collapsed due to frequent wildfires and introduced poisonous cane toads. Tasmania (-32.5%) lost least because the Bass Strait sea crossing kept out red foxes.

The Northern Monitoring Blind Spot

Comparing both maps reveals why survey gaps matter: wildlife tracking is heavily concentrated in populous eastern states (62 species tracked in New South Wales and 58 in Queensland). In contrast, the Northern Territory tracks just 29 species despite suffering Australia's second-worst wildlife collapse (-61.8%), leaving northern losses poorly tracked.

The Cold-Blooded Survey Gap

Nearly 88% of survey sites track birds (397 sites) via daytime volunteer birdwatchers and mammals (154 sites) via coastal camera traps. Although amphibians (44 sites) and reptiles (35 sites) suffered the worst collapses in Section 1, vast inland desert regions remain unmonitored because nocturnal species require specialist trapping.

Eastern Hotspots Outrank State Area

Threatened wildlife follow coastal forests and human cities rather than state land size. Despite being smaller than Western Australia (159 total listings), New South Wales (214) and Queensland (210) rank first and second overall, with high counts across birds (91 & 73), mammals (57 & 64), reptiles (37 & 46), and frogs (29 & 27).

Why Frogs & Reptiles Face the Highest Extinction Risk

Critically Endangered is the final warning stage before extinction in the wild. Today, 36.7% of listed amphibians and 26.2% of reptiles sit in this top-risk tier, compared to only 8.0% of mammals. This is because dozens of Australia's most vulnerable mammals already went extinct in the 1800s, whereas frogs face their peak crisis today.

Overlapping Threats & Bushfires

Most threatened species face several dangers at once: invasive species (78.4%), frequent fires (59.8%), and farming (52.3%) each impact over half of all listed species. Worse, these pressures compound one another, such as when severe bushfires burn away ground shelter, leaving surviving wildlife exposed to introduced predators.

Why Rabbits & Mould Outrank Predators

While feral cats (harming 123 native species) and red foxes (95 species) hunt animals directly, plant-eating European rabbits threaten the most species overall (322) by stripping native plants. Three of the top ten invaders are non-animals, led by root-rot mould (harming 236 species) and invasive weeds (Lantana and Blackberry).

Total Area vs. Share of Habitat Lost

Measuring land clearing only in total hectares hides severe threats to animals that live in one small region. The Red Goshawk lost the largest total area (3.02 million hectares), but because this bird lives across most of Australia, that is only 2.3% of its habitat. In contrast, clearing 0.70 million hectares destroyed 10.0% of the Southern Black-throated Finch's entire habitat.

Why Complete Predator Removal Matters

Mammals provide the only long-term national dataset comparing management levels. Between 2000 and 2017, unmanaged wild populations fell by 59.3%, while partially managed sites (such as periodic pest baiting) grew by just +28%. Inside predator-free fenced havens and islands, populations surged by +729%, proving complete predator removal is essential.

The Priority Selection Paradox

Under the 2022–2032 Action Plan, only 14.0% of listed animals (60 of 429) are chosen as national Priority Species for focused recovery action. Because the government also considers how famous and culturally iconic an animal is, well-known mammals receive the highest share of priority spots (18.6%), while less famous amphibians (12.2%) and reptiles (10.7%) receive the least despite being closer to extinction.
