A. Twenty Years of Wild Decline

When Australia enacted its national environment law (the EPBC Act) in 2000, scientists set average monitored wildlife populations to a starting score of 1.0 (100%). Over the next two decades, wild populations plunged by 62.6% to 0.374, with steep early losses during the Millennium Drought (2001–2009).

B. Legal Listing Lags Reality

When the law began in 2000, 335 animal species were already listed. Adding 94 new species between 2000 and 2020 brought the total to 429 protected animals, yet wild populations kept falling. The 2014–2016 surge reflects regulators clearing an overdue paperwork backlog rather than a sudden event.

Which Animal Groups Are Losing the Most?

The Collapse of Frogs & Reptiles

Comparing all four groups since 1985 reveals a stark divide. While monitored mammals (62% left) and birds (44% left) retained roughly half their baseline levels by 2022, cold-blooded reptiles (6%) and amphibians (3%) suffered near-total collapse due to drying wetlands, habitat loss, and an infectious amphibian skin fungus.

Winners and Losers Within Each Group

Trends diverge sharply within each group since 2000. Marine mammals (+42.9%) and large mammals (+25.7%) rebounded under commercial whaling bans and protected reserves. Conversely, land-breeding frogs (-95.2%) and freshwater reptiles (-76.5%) collapsed from inland river drying, while mid-sized mammals (-47.9%) fell prey to feral cats and foxes.

SECTION 2 OF 5: GEOGRAPHIC BLIND SPOTS

Where Wildlife Is Vanishing vs. Where Scientists Look

💡 Interactive: Click any state on the maps below to highlight it across all spatial views.

Tasmania's Ocean Shield

Because this map plots relative percentage change since 2000, compact states can be compared fairly against large ones. Mainland Victoria (-62.3%) and the Northern Territory (-61.8%) fell steepest from land clearing, fires, and cane toads, whereas Tasmania (-32.5%) lost least because the Bass Strait sea crossing blocked red foxes.

Uneven Regional Tracking

Across every state, scientists actively monitor roughly 30% of legally listed wildlife. However, because populous eastern states track 62 species in New South Wales and 58 in Queensland while the Northern Territory tracks just 29 species, northern Australia's steep -61.8% collapse rests on far thinner field data.

Why Survey Coverage Varies by Group

Birds (397 sites) are counted nationwide by daytime volunteer birdwatchers, while mammals (154 sites) are tracked mainly along coastal forests and reserves using camera traps. In contrast, amphibians (44 sites) and reptiles (35 sites) show vast inland desert gaps because nocturnal desert species require specialist ground traps after rare rains.

SECTION 3 OF 5: NATIONAL PROTECTION LISTS

Where Protected Wildlife Live and Their Extinction Risk

Eastern Hotspots Outrank State Area

Threatened species follow coastal biodiversity and human settlement rather than state land size. Despite being smaller than Western Australia, New South Wales and Queensland hold the highest listed counts across all four groups: birds (91 & 73), mammals (57 & 64), reptiles (37 & 46), and over 70% of frogs (29 & 27).

Acute Risk in Cold-Blooded Groups

Critically Endangered is the final stage before extinction in the wild. Today, 36.7% of listed amphibians (18 of 49) and 26.2% of reptiles (27 of 103) sit in this highest-risk tier, compared to 11.0% of birds and 8.0% of mammals, showing that frogs and reptiles face the most immediate extinction danger.

SECTION 4 OF 5: DRIVERS OF DECLINE

What Is Pushing Australia's Wildlife Toward Extinction?

Overlapping Threats & Bushfires

Most threatened species face several dangers at once: invasive species (78.4%), frequent fires (59.8%), and farming (52.3%) each impact over half of all listed species. Worse, these pressures compound one another when severe bushfires burn away ground shelter, leaving surviving wildlife exposed to introduced predators.

Why Rabbits & Mould Outrank Predators

While feral cats (harming 123 native species) and red foxes (95 species) hunt animals directly, plant-eating European rabbits threaten the most species overall (322) by stripping native plants. Three of the top ten invaders are non-animals, led by root-rot mould (harming 236 species) and invasive weeds (Lantana and Blackberry).

Total Area vs. Share of Habitat Lost

Measuring land clearing only in total hectares hides severe impacts on small-range wildlife. The wide-ranging Red Goshawk lost the largest physical area (3.02 million hectares), equal to 2.3% of its potential continental habitat. Conversely, losing 0.70 million hectares destroyed 10.0% of the Southern Black-throated Finch's potential habitat.

SECTION 5 OF 5: CONSERVATION OUTCOMES

What Actually Works in the Wild vs. The Funding Gap

Proof That Predator Removal Works

Mammals are the only group with long-term national data comparing management regimes. From 2000 to 2017, populations inside predator-free fenced havens and islands surged by +729%. Sites with any known management grew by +28%, while unmanaged wild populations fell by 59.3%, proving extinction is reversible when feral predators are excluded.

The Priority Targeting Gap

Under the 2022–2032 Action Plan, only 14.0% of listed animals (60 of 429) are designated as national Priority Species for focused recovery action. Mammals hold the highest share (18.6%), whereas amphibians (12.2%) and reptiles (10.7%) hold the lowest share despite facing the highest critically endangered rates.
