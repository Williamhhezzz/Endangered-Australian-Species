Paper Protection vs. Real-World Loss: In 2000, Australia introduced its main national environment law (the EPBC Act) to protect threatened wildlife. To track whether populations are recovering, scientists set average wildlife numbers in the year 2000 to a starting score of 1.0 (100%). By 2020, monitored wild populations had fallen by 62.6% to a score of 0.374. Although the government added 94 more animal species to the national protection list after 2000 (bringing total protected backboned animals to 429 today), adding species to a legal list has not stopped their decline in the wild.

SECTION 1 OF 5: WINNERS AND LOSERS

Which Animal Groups Are Losing the Most?

The Silent Collapse of Frogs and Reptiles: Public campaigns mostly feature famous animals like koalas and kangaroos. However, cold-blooded animals have suffered the worst long-term losses since 1985, with only 3% of frog populations and 6% of reptile populations remaining by 2022. Because frogs breathe and drink through thin skin, they are highly sensitive to drying rivers, rising heat, and an introduced fungal skin disease called chytrid fungus.

Why Mid-Sized Mammals Are Disappearing: Since 2000, marine mammals (+42.9%) and large land mammals (+25.7%) have grown thanks to commercial whaling bans and protected reserves. In contrast, mid-sized land mammals (35g to 5.5kg) dropped by 47.9%. Native pouch-bearing mammals of this size, such as bandicoots and quolls, are the exact prey size hunted by introduced feral cats and red foxes. Meanwhile, migratory shorebirds fell by 56.4% as coastal mudflat stopovers along their migration route through Malaysia and Southeast Asia are cleared for development.

SECTION 2 OF 5: GEOGRAPHIC BLIND SPOTS

Where Wildlife Is Vanishing vs. Where Scientists Look

Tasmania's Ocean Shield vs. Mainland Losses: Every Australian state lost nearly a third or more of its monitored wildlife between 2000 and 2022. On the mainland, Victoria (-62.3%) and the Northern Territory (-61.8%) suffered the steepest drops due to historic farming land clearing, severe bushfires, and poisonous cane toads. Tasmania recorded the smallest drop (-32.5%) because the Bass Strait ocean crossing prevented European red foxes from ever establishing on the island.

The Northern Monitoring Blind Spot: Comparing the two maps side by side reveals an important gap in scientific tracking. Populous eastern states like New South Wales (62 tracked species) and Queensland (58 species) have dense survey networks near major cities and universities. In contrast, the Northern Territory tracks only 29 species despite suffering the country's second-steepest wildlife collapse (-61.8%), leaving remote northern wildlife to decline with minimal monitoring.

Why Birds Are Counted Everywhere: Why do birds (397 survey sites) have monitoring records across almost the entire continent while reptiles (35 sites) and frogs (44 sites) have huge empty gaps across central and northern Australia? Bird counts are supported by thousands of volunteer birdwatchers active during the day. In contrast, desert lizards and frogs are small, camouflaged, and active mostly at night after rare desert rains, requiring specialist scientists to set ground traps in remote outback areas.

The Eastern Coastline Hotspot: New South Wales (91 birds, 57 mammals) and Queensland (73 birds, 64 mammals) have the highest number of legally threatened species. Both states combine rich coastal forests with Australia's heaviest human population growth, city expansion, and farming land clearing. More than half of all state-listed frogs live in these two states, where cool mountain rainforest frogs have nowhere higher to move as temperatures warm.

Historical Survivor Bias: Under national law, Critically Endangered is the final warning stage before extinction in the wild. Today, 36.7% of listed frogs and 26.2% of listed reptiles sit in this highest-risk tier, compared to only 8.0% of listed mammals. This is a result of survivor bias: dozens of Australia's most fragile mammal species already went extinct in the 1800s and 1900s before modern protection laws existed, whereas frogs and reptiles are facing their extinction crisis right now.

SECTION 4 OF 5: DRIVERS OF DECLINE

What Is Pushing Australia's Wildlife Toward Extinction?

How Bushfires Help Feral Predators: Invasive species (78.4%) and severe fires (59.8%) are the top two threats to listed species, and they make each other worse. Introduced pasture weeds grow thick and dry out quickly, fuelling hot wildfires that burn away fallen logs and low bushes. Once a fire leaves the ground bare, feral cats and foxes walk into the burned areas to hunt native animals that have lost their hiding places.

Why Rabbits Outrank Predators: While feral cats (123 species) and foxes (95 species) hunt animals directly, the plant-eating European rabbit harms the most threatened species overall (322 species). Rabbits eat native seedlings down to bare soil, causing erosion and starving native burrowing animals. Second on the list is root-rot mould (Phytophthora, 236 species), an introduced soil disease that kills native forest trees from the roots up, wiping out food and shelter for wildlife.

Total Area Cleared vs. Share of Habitat Lost: Looking only at total land area cleared can hide severe threats to local species. Wide-ranging birds like the Red Goshawk lost the largest total area (3.02 million hectares across northern Australia), which equals 2.4% of their vast habitat across the continent. In contrast, the Southern Black-throated Finch lost a smaller total area (0.70 million hectares), but that destroyed 10.0% of its entire potential habitat due to grazing and coal mining in Queensland.

SECTION 5 OF 5: CONSERVATION OUTCOMES

What Actually Works in the Wild vs. The Funding Gap

The Fenced Sanctuary Success Story: When introduced cats and foxes are completely removed, native mammals recover rapidly. Between 2000 and 2017, threatened mammal populations inside predator-free fenced sanctuaries and offshore islands surged by +729% (rising from a baseline score of 1.0 to 8.29). Sites with general management (such as periodic pest baiting) grew modestly (+28%), while unmanaged wild populations fell by 59.3%. Fenced sanctuaries prove extinction can be reversed when threats are removed.

The 86% Priority Funding Gap: Although hands-on conservation clearly works, funding remains tightly limited. Under the Australian Government's 2022–2032 Threatened Species Action Plan, only 14.0% of listed backboned animals were selected as official Priority Species for dedicated recovery funding (covering 18.6% of mammals, 13.4% of birds, 12.2% of frogs, and 10.7% of reptiles). Around 86% of threatened wildlife remain outside the priority list, showing that reversing Australia's wildlife decline requires scaling up funded field protection.
